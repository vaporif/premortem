Where diverged:

1. Geography: GuavaPass operated in APAC (Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Hong Kong, Jakarta, Bangkok, Dubai); BodyVibes targets Warsaw, Poland (EU) — a market where ClassPass has historically had limited or no presence, potentially reducing the direct acquisition-and-shutdown risk that killed GuavaPass. 2. Failure causes:

- acquisition and shutdown by ClassPass in markets where ClassPass already operated
- studio payment delays — GuavaPass owed partner studios delayed payments since at least October 2018
- insufficient member notice period at shutdown — only one week, causing member backlash over unused credits and refunds
- inability to survive head-to-head competition with a better-funded global competitor (ClassPass) in overlapping markets
- dependence on a two-sided marketplace where studio payment obligations created cash-flow pressure

Lessons:

- Maintain a studio payment reserve and never delay partner payouts — late payments destroy the supply-side trust that is your core asset, as GuavaPass's October 2018 arrears demonstrated.
- Build defensible local network effects before a global competitor (ClassPass, Gympass/Wellhub) enters Warsaw; local density and studio exclusivity are your moat.
- Ensure your membership terms include clear wind-down or transfer provisions so that if you are acquired or pivot, members have adequate notice and credit protection — the one-week shutdown window created lasting PR damage for GuavaPass.
- Treat the B2B/corporate tier as a priority revenue stabiliser: enterprise contracts provide predictable cash flow that can buffer the volatility of consumer churn and studio payment obligations.
- Monitor whether ClassPass or Gympass/Wellhub is targeting Warsaw; being acquired at a discount or shut down in a market-overlap scenario is a real exit risk, not just a growth outcome — structure early investor terms accordingly.

## MoviePass

US subscription service letting consumers see one movie per day at theaters for a flat monthly fee (~$9.95), killed by unsustainable unit economics and rapid cash burn.

Failure date: 2019-01-01
Lifespan: 96 months

| market | 4.0 | MoviePass operated in the US movie-theater sector (media/entertainment), while BodyVibes targets boutique fitness studios in Warsaw, Poland. Both serve cost-conscious consumers seeking variety, but the verticals, geographies, and underlying supplier dynamics differ meaningfully. |

| stage_scale | 5.0 | MoviePass at its peak had 3M+ subscribers in the US. BodyVibes claims 2,000+ active users and 80+ studios in a single city. BodyVibes is at a far earlier, city-level stage, though both started with a single-market beta before attempting to scale. |

Why similar:

BodyVibes and MoviePass share the same core architecture: a flat-fee consumer subscription that aggregates access to a network of third-party venues, delivered via a mobile app with check-in functionality, tiered pricing (including a premium tier), and a parallel B2B/corporate channel. Both pitch 'variety over single-venue membership' and lean on savings-vs-drop-in as the primary consumer value proposition. MoviePass's history is the closest completed experiment in subscription-aggregation unit economics that BodyVibes will face.

Where diverged:

1. Vertical/sector: BodyVibes is in boutique fitness (yoga, pilates, crossfit, etc.), not movie ticketing — the supplier cost structure differs because a fitness class has marginal capacity that studios want to fill, whereas MoviePass paid full retail per ticket with no revenue share from theaters. 2. Geography: BodyVibes operates in Warsaw, Poland (EU regulatory/consumer environment), not the US. 3. Scale: BodyVibes is a single-city early-stage product (2,000+ users, 80+ studios); MoviePass was a national US rollout that hit 3M+ subscribers before collapsing. 4. Partner alignment: BodyVibes explicitly frames itself as 'partner-friendly to small studios that lack their own booking infrastructure,' suggesting a cooperative supply-side relationship; MoviePass faced open hostility from major theater chains (AMC threatened legal action). 5. Ownership structure: BodyVibes appears to be an independent founder-led company; MoviePass was acquired by an analytics firm (HMNY) whose data-monetization agenda distorted pricing decisions fatally.

Failure causes:

- unit economics negative at scale — cost per ticket exceeded subscription revenue for frequent users
- predatory pricing ($9.95/mo) designed to grow data pool, not to be sustainable
- acquirer (HMNY) prioritized subscriber growth and ad-data strategy over path to profitability
- no revenue-share agreements with theater chains, so every heavy user was a direct cash loss
- rapid cash burn (~$40M/month shortfall by May 2018) with no viable recapitalization
- deceptive throttling of heavy users (password resets, hidden trip-wires) leading to FTC action and class-action lawsuits
- competitor response (AMC Stubs A-List) commoditized the value proposition once the market was proven

Lessons:

- Model your unit economics at 3× expected usage frequency before setting subscription price — MoviePass's $9.95 price was never viable for users attending more than one movie per month.
- Negotiate a revenue-share or preferential cost arrangement with studio partners before scaling — without it, every heavy user becomes a cash drain.
- Do not use data-monetization as a secret subsidy for unsustainable pricing; build the business to be profitable on subscription revenue alone.
- Cap growth to the rate your cash reserves and per-unit margin can support — viral subscriber spikes without positive margin will accelerate, not solve, a cash crisis.
- Secure partner buy-in contractually before launch; resistance from dominant venue chains (as AMC showed) can block access and trigger a death spiral.
